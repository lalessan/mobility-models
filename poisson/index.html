How the best fit works

Modelling human mobility · DIS Copenhagen · What the Best fit button in the playground does, step by step. Same Danish data (Statistics Denmark, PEND100, 2024).

1. Every flow is a count

For each pair of municipalities, the gravity model gives an expected number of commuters, μij = K miα mjβ dij−γ. The real number will not be exactly μ: if many people each decide on their own, the count scatters around μ following a Poisson distribution. Its spread grows with μ (the variance equals the mean): an error of 5 people is a lot when you expect 10, and nothing when you expect 10,000.

The bars show how likely each count is when the model expects μ. The red bar is what we actually observed: its height is the likelihood of this pair. Move μ to make the red bar as tall as possible: you will land on μ = T.

2. Best fit = the parameters that make the data most likely

The likelihood of the whole model is the product of the red bars for all 9,506 pairs; we work with its logarithm, which turns the product into a sum. Best fit picks the α, β, γ that make this log-likelihood as high as possible (K is then set so that the total matches). Each curve below shows the log-likelihood as you vary one parameter while keeping the other two where they are. Move the sliders: the dot climbs to the top of its curve when that parameter is at its best value.

"Run the fit" starts from Zipf and uses the standard algorithm for Poisson regression (iteratively reweighted least squares). Each step solves a weighted regression in which big expected flows get big weights. It usually lands on the best values in five or six steps.

3. Why not a straight line through the logs?

Taking logs turns the gravity model into a linear regression, log Tij = log K + α log mi + β log mj − γ log dij, so it is tempting to fit it with ordinary least squares. On these data that goes badly:

Two reasons. Zeros: 1,079 pairs have no commuters at all, and log 0 does not exist, so least squares has to drop them. Weights: on a log scale, being off by a factor of two counts the same for 6 vs 3 commuters as for 6,000 vs 3,000. Half of all pairs have fewer than 10 commuters, so these tiny flows dominate the fit, and the big flows, where most people are, are fitted badly. The Poisson likelihood keeps the zeros and lets each pair count according to how many people it involves. Press Least squares on logs above to see how far down the curves it sits.

Further reading: Silva & Tenreyro (2006), "The log of gravity", Review of Economics and Statistics, which made this argument for trade flows between countries. The notebook on the lecture page does the same fit with statsmodels.
